In [cataract surgery](@/learn/cataract-surgery.md) the cloudy natural lens is replaced by a clear artificial one, an **intraocular lens** or implant. The implant stays in the eye for life. Its type and its power are chosen before surgery, and together they decide what you will see clearly without glasses afterward: far, arm's length, near, or some of each.

## Monofocal lenses

A **monofocal** lens focuses at one distance. It is the standard lens, the one most people receive, and it is usually covered by insurance. Vision at its set distance is very sharp and high in contrast, and it gives the fewest problems with glare at night.

- **Set for distance**, the most common choice: driving, television and faces across a room are clear without glasses. Reading glasses are needed for near, and arm's length (a computer or the car dashboard) is usable but softer.
- **Set for near:** reading is clear without glasses, and glasses are worn for distance. Some people who have been nearsighted all their lives, and are used to reading without glasses, prefer this.

You may hear of **enhanced monofocal** lenses, marketed as giving a little more range at arm's length than a standard monofocal. In practice they work like a standard monofocal: any extra range is small and not something to count on, so they are not worth paying extra for. If you want real range without glasses, the options are [monovision](#monovision), an [extended depth](#extended-depth-of-focus-lenses) or [multifocal](#multifocal-lenses) lens, or a [light adjustable lens](#light-adjustable-lenses).

## Monovision

**Monovision** uses monofocal lenses with one eye set for distance and the other for near (or arm's length). The brain learns to use whichever eye is clear for the task. Done well, it gives as much freedom from glasses as a multifocal lens, with a good range from distance to near, and without the halos or loss of contrast. The trade-offs are slightly less depth perception and a little less sharpness with both eyes together, and glasses may still help for night driving or long reading. It is not for everyone: the two eyes no longer work together the same way. Many people love it, but some cannot get used to it. It suits people who have used monovision with contact lenses and liked it. If you have not, try it with contact lenses before surgery; because the result is permanent, surgery should not be the first time. A [light adjustable lens](#light-adjustable-lenses) is another way to try it, since it can be changed after surgery.

## Toric lenses

**Astigmatism** means the front of the eye is curved more like a football than a basketball, which smears vision in one direction. A **toric** lens has astigmatism correction built in. It has small marks at its edge so the surgeon can turn it to line up with your astigmatism. Without it, an eye with significant astigmatism would need glasses for clear distance vision even after surgery. Small amounts, up to about 1 diopter, are usually corrected instead with curved laser cuts in the cornea during [laser-assisted surgery](#laser-assisted-cataract-surgery), without a toric lens.

Toric versions exist of most kinds of lens, including extended depth and multifocal lenses, and are used whenever one of those is chosen for an eye with significant astigmatism, since leaving the astigmatism would undo much of the benefit. A toric lens can occasionally rotate in the first weeks and need a small procedure to turn it back.

## Extended depth of focus lenses

An **extended depth of focus** (EDOF) lens stretches the range of clear vision from distance to arm's length. Most people can drive, use a computer and see a dashboard without glasses, and use light reading glasses for small print. Some notice mild halos or glare around lights at night, far less than with a multifocal lens. Designs differ: some use fine rings, like a multifocal, and give more near vision with more halos; others are shaped without rings and give fewer halos with a little less near vision.

## Multifocal lenses

A **multifocal** lens (bifocal or trifocal) splits light between distances using fine rings on its surface, so far, arm's length and near can all be usable without glasses. They give the most freedom from glasses, with trade-offs:

- **Halos or rings around lights at night**, and sometimes glare. Most people adapt over months, but some remain bothered.
- **Slightly less contrast**, because the light is shared. This matters in dim light, and it matters more when the eye has another condition that reduces contrast.
- **They depend on a healthy eye.** A multifocal lens works best when the cornea, the macula and the optic nerve are healthy.

## Light adjustable lenses

A **light adjustable lens** is a monofocal lens whose power can be changed after surgery. Once the eye has healed, usually two to three weeks later, you try out the vision. Then, over a few short visits, the surgeon shines a measured pattern of ultraviolet light on the lens through a dilated pupil to fine-tune its power, correcting leftover nearsightedness, farsightedness or astigmatism. A final treatment locks the power in for good.

- **It is the surest way to land the target**, whether that is sharp distance, near, or monovision, because the power is checked and adjusted after surgery rather than predicted beforehand.
- **You can try the result before it is final.** One eye can be set a little for near, so you can test monovision ("blended vision") and change your mind.
- **It helps when the power is hard to predict**, such as after LASIK, PRK or RK.
- **UV-protective glasses** must be worn during all waking hours, indoors and out, from surgery until a day after the final lock-in, usually some weeks. Ordinary sunglasses are not enough: daylight could change the lens unpredictably.
- **It is not for everyone.** It is not used with macular disease, with medicines that make the skin or eyes sensitive to light, after a herpes infection of the eye, with eyes that shake (nystagmus), or when the pupil does not dilate well enough for the treatments.

Like other lenses aimed at reducing glasses, it usually carries an extra charge.

## When a simpler lens is wiser

A monofocal or toric lens is usually recommended when the eye has, or is likely to develop, a condition that reduces contrast or affects the center of vision:

- [Glaucoma](@/learn/what-is-glaucoma.md), especially moderate or advanced, since glaucoma already lowers contrast and future visual field tests are easiest to interpret with a monofocal lens. Extended depth lenses are sometimes considered in mild, stable glaucoma.
- [Macular degeneration](@/learn/macular-degeneration.md), [diabetic eye disease](@/learn/diabetic-retinopathy.md) or a membrane on the retina
- Significant [dry eye](@/learn/what-is-dry-eye.md) or corneal disease such as [Fuchs dystrophy](@/learn/fuchs-dystrophy.md) or [keratoconus](@/learn/keratoconus.md)
- Previous laser vision correction (LASIK or PRK), where the lens power is harder to predict (a light adjustable lens is one answer to that)
- A lazy eye (amblyopia), which gains little from a lens that splits focus
- A lot of night driving, or work that needs the sharpest possible contrast

## How the power is chosen

{{<figure src="images/learn/iol-measure.svg" alt="The eye cut in half, with an arrow along its length from the cornea to the retina, and a dashed curve following the front of the cornea." caption="Before surgery the length of the eye and the curve of the cornea are measured. Together they set the power of the lens implant. Simplified, not to scale." />}}

Measurements taken before surgery, mainly the **length of the eye** and the **curve of the cornea**, go into formulas that pick the implant's power. They are very accurate, but not perfect: a small difference from the target is common and is corrected with glasses. The measurements are less certain after LASIK, PRK or RK, or with a very long or short eye. If you have had one of those, bring any records you have, especially your glasses prescription from before it. Contact lenses change the shape of the cornea, so you may be asked to leave them out for some days before measuring. [Preparing for Cataract Surgery](@/learn/preparing-for-cataract-surgery.md) covers this and the rest of the lead-up.

## Glasses afterward

Even with the best result, most people use glasses for something: reading glasses with a monofocal lens, or glasses for fine print, night driving or long computer work with the others. Your final prescription is checked about a month after surgery, once the eye has settled.

## Laser-assisted cataract surgery

In **laser-assisted cataract surgery**, a femtosecond laser does some of the first steps before the surgeon takes over: it makes the round opening in the front of the lens capsule, the small incisions, and cuts that soften the cataract so less ultrasound is needed to remove it. It can also make small curved cuts in the cornea that correct a little astigmatism, up to about 1 diopter, without a toric lens; more than that needs a toric lens. The rest of the operation is the same.

- **With a toric, extended depth, multifocal or light adjustable lens**, and for a little astigmatism, the laser is usually part of the surgery. A precisely sized and centered opening helps these lenses sit where they should.
- **With Fuchs dystrophy or a very dense cataract**, needing less ultrasound energy is gentler on the cornea.
- **For most eyes, it does not give better vision.** Large trials comparing it with standard surgery found that, on average, vision and safety afterward are about the same.
- **It is not used** when the pupil does not dilate well enough, a corneal scar would block the laser, the laser's suction ring could press on a trabeculectomy bleb or tube shunt, or you cannot lie flat and still for a few minutes. Standard surgery works well in those eyes.

It adds a few minutes to the visit, and it usually carries an extra charge, often included in the price of the lens.

## Cost

In the United States, a standard monofocal lens is covered by Medicare and most insurance. Toric, extended depth, multifocal and light adjustable lenses usually carry an extra out-of-pocket charge, because they are aimed at reducing the need for glasses, and so does laser-assisted surgery. The amount varies, so ask for the cost in advance.
