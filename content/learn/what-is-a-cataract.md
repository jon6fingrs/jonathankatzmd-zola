A **cataract** is clouding of the eye's natural lens, the clear lens that sits behind the pupil and focuses light onto the retina. Almost everyone develops some cataract with age. It happens slowly, usually over years, and it is painless. It becomes a problem when it gets in the way of seeing clearly, and then it is treated with [cataract surgery](@/learn/cataract-surgery.md), which replaces the cloudy lens with a clear one.

## What a cataract does to vision

A clear lens lets light pass straight through to a sharp focus on the retina. A cloudy lens blocks some of that light and scatters some of it, so less of the picture arrives in focus and stray light washes over the rest. That causes:

- Blurry or dim vision, like looking through a dirty or foggy window
- Glare and halos around lights, especially headlights at night
- Colors that look faded or yellowed
- Needing more light to read
- Frequent changes in your glasses prescription
- A ghost or double image in one eye, even with the other eye covered

A cataract does not cause pain or redness. A painful or red eye, or vision that changes suddenly over hours or days, is something else and needs a call the same day: see [When to call the same day](@/learn/when-to-call.md).

## The three common kinds

Many people have more than one kind at once.

**Nuclear cataract** is the most common. The center of the lens, the nucleus, slowly hardens and turns yellow, then brown. Vision dims and blurs, especially in the distance, and colors lose their brightness. It often makes the eye more nearsighted, so some people find they can read without their glasses for a while. This "second sight" does not last, because the cataract keeps growing.

**Cortical cataract** starts at the edge of the lens, as white spokes that point toward the center. The spokes scatter light, so glare and halos are the main complaints. They bother people most at night, when the pupil is wide open and light passes through the edge of the lens.

**Posterior subcapsular cataract** is a small cloudy plaque on the back surface of the lens, right in the center, where all the light passes. Even a small one causes glare and blurry reading vision, especially in bright light, when the pupil is small and all the light has to go through the plaque. It tends to grow faster than the other kinds, sometimes over months, and it is more common in younger people, in people with diabetes, and with steroid medicines.

## Who gets cataracts

Age is the main cause: most people over 60 have some cataract, and nearly everyone does by 80. Other things make cataracts come earlier or grow faster:

- Diabetes
- Steroid medicines, as pills, high-dose inhalers or eye drops, especially when used for a long time
- Smoking
- Many years of strong sunlight
- An injury to the eye
- Earlier eye surgery, such as [vitrectomy](@/learn/vitrectomy.md)
- Family history, and some inherited conditions; rarely, a baby is born with a cataract

## How it is found

Your eye doctor sees a cataract by looking at the lens through a dilated pupil at the slit lamp, the same view as the front view in the demo. A vision check shows how much it affects your sight. When glare is the main complaint, vision may also be measured with a bright light shining toward the eye, because a cataract can look mild on a standard chart and still be disabling in sunlight or at night.

## When to treat it

Early on, new glasses, brighter reading lights and good sunglasses may be all that is needed. No eye drop, supplement or exercise clears a cataract or stops it from growing.

Surgery makes sense when the cataract gets in the way of what you need or want to do: driving, especially at night; reading; recognizing faces; working; or meeting the vision requirement for your driver's license. There is no need to wait for a cataract to be "ripe", and in most cases no harm in waiting while it is not bothering you. Your doctor may suggest not waiting if the cataract is making glaucoma harder to control or narrowing the drain inside the eye, or if it is so dense that it hides the retina and optic nerve from view. [Cataract Surgery](@/learn/cataract-surgery.md) covers the operation and recovery, and [Choosing a Lens Implant](@/learn/lens-implants.md) the choice of lens.

## Cataract and glaucoma

As the lens grows thicker with age, it pushes the iris forward. In eyes that already have a narrow space between the iris and the cornea, that can crowd the eye's drain and raise the pressure: see [Narrow Angles and Angle Closure](@/learn/angle-closure.md). Removing the cataract makes more room and often lowers the pressure a little. If you have glaucoma, cataract surgery can often be combined with a [minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS)](@/learn/migs.md) in the same visit.

## Can cataracts be prevented?

Not completely, because they are part of aging. You can slow them down:

- Wear sunglasses that block UV light, and a hat with a brim, outdoors
- Do not smoke
- Keep diabetes well controlled
- Use steroid medicines only as prescribed, and ask whether a lower dose or a different medicine would work
